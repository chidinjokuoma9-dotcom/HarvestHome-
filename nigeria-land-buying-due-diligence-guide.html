Buying Land in Nigeria: A Practical Due-Diligence Checklist

Prepared for buyers, sellers and marketplace visitors. General information only; requirements can vary by state, product and transaction.

Land advertisements can help you discover opportunities, but a listing, photograph, location pin or seller's promise cannot establish ownership. Before paying a deposit or signing an agreement, investigate the particular land and the seller's authority through independent professionals and the appropriate state offices.

Key principle: Do not treat a document as verified simply because you have seen a photograph or photocopy. Ask a Nigerian property lawyer and a registered surveyor to check the documents and the physical site independently. Processes and records differ between states.

1. Identify exactly what is being offered

  • Write down the full location, estate or community name, plot or parcel number, approximate area and the seller's stated boundaries.
  • Ask whether the offer is for a whole parcel, a subdivided plot, an allocation, an assignment of an existing interest, or another arrangement.
  • Ask for a current survey plan and the title documents the seller relies on. Names, dates, plot references, dimensions and locations should be consistent across the papers.
  • Visit the site. A map pin or video call is not a substitute for seeing the land with your surveyor.

2. Establish who owns it and who can sign

Ask the seller to explain how the land came into their possession and provide the documents supporting that history. Your lawyer should trace the root of title and investigate the chain of transactions relevant to the property. Depending on the circumstances, the seller may be an individual, company, family, estate, trustee or authorised representative.

  • Compare the seller's identity with the names appearing in the title documents and investigate any difference.
  • If an agent or representative is involved, establish the scope and validity of their authority. Do not assume that possession of keys or documents gives an agent the right to sell.
  • For family or inherited property, ask your lawyer to establish the correct persons and consents required for a valid transaction.
  • For a company seller, verify the company and the authority of the people signing on its behalf.

3. Understand the documents instead of relying on their names

Documents encountered in Nigerian land transactions may include a Certificate of Occupancy, a registered deed, an allocation or grant document, a survey plan, probate or letters of administration, a power of attorney, or family and community records. Which documents matter depends on the land's history and the interest being transferred. No single document should be treated as a universal guarantee.

Ask your lawyer to explain what each document establishes, whether it is genuine and properly registered where required, whether the person named has authority to transfer the interest, and whether earlier transactions or missing approvals create a problem. Ask for the original documents to be inspected through a safe, documented process; do not take possession of originals without professional advice.

4. Have the survey and site checked independently

  • Engage a registered surveyor to compare the survey plan with the actual site and explain the coordinates, dimensions, beacons and adjoining parcels.
  • Ask the surveyor to carry out the appropriate charting or checks with the relevant Surveyor-General's office.
  • Walk the boundaries on site with the surveyor where practicable. Look for encroachment, overlapping claims, missing or moved beacons, occupied areas and disagreement with neighbouring plots.
  • Check practical access, drainage, flooding or erosion signs, terrain, utility availability and any visible restrictions that may affect your intended use.

5. Request official searches and planning checks

Your lawyer should identify the searches available for that state and parcel. These may include a search at the state land registry, checks for registered interests or encumbrances, litigation enquiries where appropriate, and enquiries about government acquisition or restrictions. Ask for the date, office, reference number and written result of each search. A verbal assurance is difficult to audit later.

Also check whether the intended use is permitted under applicable planning and land-use controls. A land title and permission to build are different questions. If you intend to build, ask the relevant planning authority and qualified professionals what approvals and site conditions apply.

6. Inspect the land and ask people nearby

Visit at a sensible time and, where possible, in different weather conditions. Check whether the site is accessible in the rainy season, whether water collects, whether there are nearby drains or waterways, and whether the actual surroundings match the seller's description. Ask neighbours or relevant community representatives about boundary disagreements, occupation, previous sales and access routes. Treat informal comments as leads to investigate, not as proof.

7. Put the commercial terms in writing

Before transferring money, have your lawyer prepare or review written terms that identify the parties, exact property, purchase price, deposit, payment milestones, documents to be delivered, completion conditions, possession date, responsibility for fees and what happens if a search reveals a problem. Make payments to the verified party through a traceable channel and retain receipts. Avoid cash handovers without proper documentation.

8. Plan for completion and registration

Ask your lawyer to explain the steps that apply after signing, including any required consent, stamping, registration, tax or other perfection steps, who is responsible for each, the expected timing and the evidence you should receive. Do not assume the process is identical in every state or for every type of interest.

Warning signs that deserve a pause

  • The seller refuses independent searches, site inspection or professional involvement.
  • The price is conditional on paying immediately or keeping the offer secret.
  • The plot shown on site does not match the survey or title description.
  • Different people claim authority to sell, or names and documents do not reconcile.
  • The seller asks you to pay an unrelated person's account without a documented explanation.
  • There are signs of occupation, disputes, acquisition, encroachment or unclear access that have not been resolved.

Take-this-to-your-professionals checklist

  • Seller's full identity and evidence of authority
  • Title history and copies of relevant documents
  • Survey plan and independent surveyor's report
  • Site inspection and boundary confirmation
  • Land registry and other relevant official search results
  • Planning and intended-use enquiries
  • Written sale agreement and payment schedule
  • Completion, consent and registration plan

Useful official starting points

Some states provide online land information services. For example, the Lagos State Lands Bureau and e-GIS portal provides property-search and land-administration services for Lagos. This is a state-specific service, not a national land registry; users should identify the correct authority for the state where the land is located.
