Buying and Selling Farm Produce in Nigeria: A Practical Trade Checklist

Prepared for buyers, sellers and marketplace visitors. General information only; requirements can vary by state, product and transaction.

A successful farm produce transaction starts with clear specifications. A buyer may say they want maize, rice, beans, cassava, palm produce, vegetables or fruit, but the seller needs to know the required variety, quality, quantity, packaging, delivery point and timing. Agreeing these details before harvest, collection or payment helps reduce avoidable disputes.

Important: Quality requirements depend on the crop, its intended use, the buyer's specification and whether the produce is for local sale, processing or export. Do not apply one moisture, grade or storage rule to every commodity. For food safety and export transactions, consult the relevant technical and regulatory authorities.

1. Describe the produce precisely

Instead of posting only “maize for sale” or “fresh tomatoes available”, include the details a serious buyer needs:

  • Crop or product name and variety, where known.
  • Harvest or production period and current availability.
  • Quantity available and the unit used: kilograms, tonnes, bags, baskets, crates or another clearly defined measure.
  • Whether the quantity is already harvested, processed, dried, sorted or still in the field.
  • Visible condition, maturity, size range, grade or quality description, and any known defects.
  • Packaging type, bag or crate size, and whether containers are included in the price.
  • Collection location, delivery options, earliest dispatch date and expected handling time.

2. Agree what “quality” means before agreeing the price

Quality is not a single visual judgement. For grains and other stored crops, buyers may care about cleanliness, foreign material, damaged or discoloured produce, insect activity, odour, moisture and consistency. For fresh fruit and vegetables, maturity, size, firmness, bruising, freshness, sorting and time since harvest may matter. For roots and tubers, size, maturity, physical damage, decay and intended processing use may be important.

Write down the buyer's acceptance criteria in plain language. If a formal grade or laboratory test is required, identify the standard, testing method, sampling procedure and who will pay for inspection before the goods move.

3. Use a fair sampling and weighing process

  • Agree when and where the produce will be sampled: at the farm, collection point, warehouse or destination.
  • For bulk or bagged produce, agree how many bags or portions will be selected and how samples will represent the whole lot.
  • Use a suitable, checked scale and agree whether the weight includes bags, crates, pallets or other packaging.
  • Record the number of units, net weight, sample observations, date, location and names of the people present.
  • If quality is disputed, agree in advance whether an independent inspector or laboratory will decide and how the cost will be shared.

4. Handle moisture and storage carefully

Excess moisture can contribute to deterioration, mould and other quality problems in some stored commodities. The safe moisture range depends on the specific crop and storage conditions. Where moisture is important, use an appropriate meter or qualified testing service and record the result rather than relying on touch alone.

Keep produce clean, dry where required, protected from rain, pests, animals, chemicals and direct ground contact, and stored in suitable conditions for that commodity. Separate damaged or suspect produce from sound stock. Use pallets or raised storage where appropriate, maintain ventilation where the crop requires it, and inspect stored goods regularly. Do not mix old and new stock without telling the buyer.

5. Plan packaging and transport before harvest or dispatch

Packaging should protect the produce during loading, stacking and travel. Choose bags, crates, cartons or bulk arrangements suitable for the commodity. Agree who supplies packaging, whether it must be clean or new, how units are labelled, and how damaged packaging will be handled.

For perishable produce, estimate the time from harvest to collection and from collection to delivery. Agree the vehicle type, loading method, protection from heat or rain, ventilation or temperature control where needed, route, delivery window and who bears the risk if the vehicle is delayed. The National eAgriculture Portal's produce-transport information is one official starting point for understanding transport considerations.

6. Calculate the full delivered cost

Compare offers using the same unit and delivery basis. A low farm-gate price may not be the lowest final cost after packaging, loading, transport, handling, storage losses, inspection, market charges and other agreed expenses. Write down whether the price is per bag, kilogram, tonne, crate or lot, and specify the exact weight or capacity of each unit.

7. Put the order and payment terms in writing

For a small transaction, a clear message or receipt may record the essentials. For a large or recurring supply, use a written purchase or supply agreement reviewed where appropriate. Include:

  • Names and contact details of buyer and seller.
  • Commodity, variety, quality specification and agreed quantity.
  • Price, unit, currency and whether transport or packaging is included.
  • Sampling, weighing, inspection and rejection procedure.
  • Collection or delivery location, schedule and responsibility for transport.
  • Deposit, balance payment, payment deadline and refund or short-delivery arrangements.
  • How shortages, spoilage, delays and quality disputes will be resolved.

Use traceable payment methods, confirm the recipient independently and keep transfer records, receipts, weigh tickets and delivery notes. Do not release a large consignment solely on the basis of an unverifiable payment screenshot.

8. Special care for export or regulated supply

Export transactions may require formal grading, inspection, fumigation, packaging, weight checks, certificates and compliance with destination-market requirements. The Federal Produce Inspection Service describes inspection and quality-control functions for agricultural produce, including export-related checks. Its quality-control information discusses issues such as sampling and moisture testing. Confirm current requirements directly with FPIS and the relevant authorities before preparing an export shipment.

For post-harvest handling and storage information, the Nigerian Stored Products Research Institute provides information on storage technologies and services. Recommendations are commodity- and condition-specific; seek advice appropriate to the produce you handle.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using vague units such as “one bag” without stating its actual weight or size.
  • Agreeing on quality only after the produce has arrived at the buyer's location.
  • Failing to inspect or sample a bulk lot before dispatch.
  • Mixing different grades or moisture conditions without disclosure.
  • Ignoring packaging, loading time, heat, rain or delays for perishable goods.
  • Calculating profit without including transport, handling, packaging and likely losses.
  • Releasing goods based only on a payment screenshot or pressure from an unknown intermediary.

Farm produce transaction checklist

  • Commodity, variety, quantity and unit clearly stated
  • Quality criteria and sampling process agreed
  • Weight and packaging tare explained
  • Moisture or other tests identified where relevant
  • Storage and pest-protection arrangements suitable for the crop
  • Transport, timing and delivery responsibility confirmed
  • Total cost and payment schedule written down
  • Inspection, rejection and dispute process agreed
  • Receipts, weigh tickets and delivery records retained
